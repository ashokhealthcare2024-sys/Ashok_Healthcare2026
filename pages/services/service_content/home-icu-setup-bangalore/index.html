What is a home ICU setup?

A home ICU setup is a bedroom fitted with the critical care equipment a patient needs, such as an ICU bed, patient monitor, oxygen supply, suction machine and, if prescribed, a ventilator, together with nurses trained to use it. It is used for patients who need ongoing intensive monitoring or breathing support, when their doctor considers home care safe and appropriate.

Families usually consider a home ICU after a long hospital stay. The patient may have a tracheostomy, may be dependent on a ventilator at night, or may need continuous monitoring after a neurological event. The hospital team may feel that the patient is stable enough to leave, or the family may wish to care for a loved one at home for comfort or end-of-life reasons.

A home ICU does not replace a hospital ICU. It works only when the patient's condition is stable enough, a doctor remains in charge of treatment, and there is a clear plan for what to do if the patient deteriorates. Our role is to provide the equipment, the trained nursing team and the coordination to make that plan work at home.

Critical care nursing at home

Equipment is only half of a home ICU. The other half is the nursing team. Our critical care nurses are assigned to tracheostomy and ventilator cases based on their experience with these procedures.

  • Airway care, tracheostomy care and suctioning.
  • Ventilator and BiPAP monitoring, including responding to alarms.
  • IV medicines through pumps, fluid balance charting and feeding.
  • Hourly or two-hourly observations, as the doctor requires.
  • Positioning, skin care and pressure sore prevention.
  • Escalation to the doctor and emergency services when needed.

Who stays responsible for treatment decisions?

The patient's treating doctor, usually the intensivist, pulmonologist or physician who managed the hospital stay, remains responsible for medical decisions. Our nurses follow the doctor's written orders and share observations and charts with the doctor.

Which patients are suitable for a home ICU?

Suitability is a medical decision, but home ICU care is commonly considered for:

  • Patients with a long-term tracheostomy, with or without ventilator support.
  • Patients who need BiPAP or ventilator support for part of the day or night.
  • Patients recovering slowly after a stroke, head injury or prolonged ICU stay, who need close monitoring.
  • Patients with advanced illness who need intensive comfort care at home.

Patients whose blood pressure, oxygen levels or heart rhythm are changing rapidly usually need to remain in hospital.

How a home ICU is set up

  1. Discussion with the treating team. We ask for the discharge plan, prescription and the doctor's view on suitability for home care.
  2. Home assessment. We check the room size, access for the bed, power points, power backup and ventilation.
  3. Plan and quote. We confirm the equipment list, nursing shifts and costs with the family.
  4. Installation. Our technicians deliver, install and test the equipment before the patient arrives.
  5. Patient transfer. The patient is brought home, usually by ambulance, and handed over to the home ICU nurse.
  6. Ongoing care. Nurses keep observation charts; technical support is available if equipment needs attention, and the plan is reviewed with the doctor.
  7. Emergency plan. The family, nurse and doctor agree in advance which hospital to use and how to transfer the patient if their condition worsens.

Frequently asked questions

Is a home ICU safe?

It can be safe for a stable patient when a doctor remains in charge, the equipment is reliable, trained nurses are present and there is a clear emergency plan. It is not suitable for every patient, and the treating doctor's advice should guide the decision.

How long does it take to set up an ICU at home?

It depends on the equipment needed and the location. Planning before the discharge date helps everything be ready when the patient arrives.

How much does a home ICU cost in Bangalore?

The cost depends on the equipment list and the number of nursing shifts. We provide an itemised quote after the assessment.

Do we need a separate room?

A room with space for the bed and equipment on both sides, a stable power supply and good ventilation is recommended. We check this during the home assessment.

What happens during a power cut?

Critical equipment should run on battery or power backup, and a backup oxygen cylinder is kept ready. We will explain the backup arrangement during setup.

Can a ventilator patient be cared for at home?

Yes, in selected cases where the doctor agrees and trained nurses are present round the clock. The ventilator settings are prescribed and adjusted only by the doctor.

Can we rent only the equipment without nurses?

Some equipment, such as a bed or oxygen concentrator, can be rented alone. For ventilator and tracheostomy patients, we strongly recommend trained nursing support.

How do you handle equipment faults?

Equipment is tested before installation. If an alarm or fault occurs, the nurse follows the safety steps and contacts our technical team for help or replacement.

How long can a home ICU continue?

For as long as it is clinically appropriate. Some patients step down to simpler care within weeks; others need long-term support. The plan is reviewed regularly with the doctor.
