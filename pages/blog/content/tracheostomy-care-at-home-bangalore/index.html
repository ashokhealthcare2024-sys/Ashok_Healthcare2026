Tracheostomy Care at Home: Essential Safety Guidelines for Families

Bringing a loved one home with a tracheostomy can feel daunting. A tracheostomy creates an opening in the neck into the windpipe, so the patient and family need a clear care plan, the right supplies and support from clinicians experienced in airway care.

This article is general education only. Tracheostomy care and suctioning are clinical procedures; follow the patient's written care plan and have procedures performed only by a qualified clinician or a caregiver specifically trained by the care team.

Understanding the tracheostomy tube

Tracheostomy tubes can have different designs and parts. Depending on the tube, there may be an outer tube, a removable inner cannula, a cuff, and a neck plate or flange. The patient's clinician should explain which parts are present and what the family is allowed to handle. Never remove, adjust or replace a tube component unless the care team has taught you to do so and included it in the care plan.

Because air enters through the opening rather than through the nose and mouth, the patient's humidification, cleaning and equipment needs can differ from those of other patients. The treating clinician should set an individual plan.

Prepare a safe bedside care area

Ask the discharge team to provide a written list of prescribed equipment and supplies, explain how to check that equipment is working, and give the family a clear emergency contact plan. Keep the care area clean, dry and free of clutter, and store supplies in the way the clinical team recommends.

  • Keep the prescribed suction equipment and backup arrangements accessible, and ask the nurse to demonstrate its safe use.
  • Keep contact details for the treating team and local emergency services nearby.
  • Follow the clinician's instructions for replacement supplies and safe disposal.
  • Do not use a device, medicine or solution in the airway unless it is specifically prescribed.

Suctioning and airway procedures require training

Suctioning may be needed when secretions interfere with breathing, but it can irritate or injure the airway if performed incorrectly or unnecessarily. It should be done only according to the patient's care plan by a trained person. The care team should demonstrate the procedure, explain when it is indicated for that patient, and explain when to stop and call for help.

This guide intentionally does not provide suction pressures, insertion depths, timing, saline instructions or tube reinsertion steps. Those decisions depend on the patient, tube and clinical plan. Do not attempt invasive airway procedures based only on online instructions.

Stoma and skin care

Follow the nurse's instructions for keeping the skin around the stoma clean and dry, changing dressings and securing the tube. Ask the care team to show the family which products to use and what should not be applied. Do not cut dressings or place loose material near the opening unless the nurse has instructed you how to do it safely.

Contact the clinician if the skin becomes increasingly red, swollen, painful or wet, if there is new bleeding, or if discharge changes or develops an unpleasant smell.

Home environment and humidification

Dust, smoke and dry air can irritate the airway and may make secretions harder to manage. Keep the patient's room as clean and smoke-free as possible. Use humidification, filters or nebulised treatments only if they are prescribed, and ask the clinician how to maintain the specific equipment.

When to get urgent help

Call 108 or seek emergency care if the patient is struggling to breathe, turns blue or grey, becomes suddenly unresponsive, or has another severe or rapidly worsening symptom. If the tube becomes displaced or blocked, follow the patient's emergency plan and call the clinical team or emergency services. Do not force a tube back into place unless you are specifically trained and instructed to do so.

For new fever, worsening secretions, persistent bleeding or concerning skin changes, contact the treating clinician promptly.

Home nurse or attendant: understanding the difference

A family caregiver or attendant can help with everyday non-clinical support within their training. Airway procedures, suctioning and tracheostomy tube changes require appropriate clinical training and must follow the treating clinician's plan. Ask the provider about the assigned professional's credentials, scope of practice and availability rather than assuming every caregiver can perform these procedures.

Frequently asked questions

How often should suctioning be done?

There is no one schedule that is appropriate for every patient. Follow the patient's written plan and training from the clinician; contact the care team if you are unsure when suctioning is needed.

What signs around the stoma should I report?

Report new or worsening redness, swelling, pain, bleeding, unusual discharge, unpleasant smell or fever to the clinical team.

What should I do if the tube comes out?

Follow the patient's emergency plan and call the trained care team or emergency services. Do not attempt reinsertion unless you have been specifically trained and instructed.

Can a patient speak or eat with a tracheostomy?

It depends on the patient's condition, tube and swallowing safety. The treating clinician or speech-language pathologist should assess before oral feeding or use of a speaking valve.

Need help planning care at home?

Talk to our team about home nursing and equipment options. The treating clinician remains responsible for the patient's care plan.
