Bedsore Prevention at Home: A Family Guide

When a person spends much of the day in bed or a chair, pressure and friction can injure skin. A prevention plan is personal: ask the treating clinician or nurse to assess the patient's risks and demonstrate care suited to their condition.

Written by Ashok Healthcare clinical team · Last updated: 2026-11-11

Disclaimer: This is general education, not a diagnosis or treatment plan. Follow the patient's clinician's instructions and seek medical assessment for new or worsening skin changes.

Understand who may be at risk

Pressure injuries, often called bedsores, can develop when pressure is not relieved from an area of skin. Risk may be higher when a person has limited movement, reduced sensation, poor circulation, moisture from incontinence, a serious illness, or difficulty eating and drinking. A person may not feel or notice early damage, so families should ask a nurse to explain the specific risk factors and what to observe. Risk can change after an illness or hospital discharge; reassess the plan when mobility, alertness, continence, or appetite changes.

Make a personalized position-change plan

There is no single turning schedule that is appropriate for every patient. Ask the care team how often the person should change position, what positions are safe, and how to protect joints, tubes, and painful areas. The plan may differ between bed and chair, and it should account for comfort, sleep, breathing needs, and any restrictions after surgery. Use pillows or positioning aids only as demonstrated. Avoid dragging a person across sheets; ask for transfer training or equipment advice if moving them is difficult.

A written chart can make shared care more consistent. It may record the time, position, comfort, and any skin concern according to the nurse's instructions. The chart is a communication aid, not a substitute for clinical assessment. If the person cannot tolerate a planned position or becomes distressed, pause and contact the clinician for an alternative rather than forcing the movement.

Check skin gently and regularly

Ask a nurse to show you which areas need checking and how often. Common pressure points include heels, ankles, hips, tailbone, elbows, and the back of the head, but the right checks depend on how the person sits and lies. Look for persistent redness or darker discoloration, warmth, swelling, tenderness, a blister, or broken skin. Skin tone can affect how redness appears, so notice changes in temperature, texture, pain, or firmness too. Do not massage a reddened or injured area; request professional advice.

During a check, maintain privacy and warmth. Use good lighting and explain each step. If an area looks different from usual, note when you first saw it, where it is, whether it is intact, and any symptoms. A photo may help a clinician compare changes only when the patient consents and it is stored privately. Do not wait for an open wound before asking for help.

Manage moisture and friction safely

Keep skin clean and dry using the cleansing routine recommended for the patient. After incontinence, clean gently and change damp clothing or bedding promptly. Ask the clinician whether a barrier product is suitable; products are not interchangeable for every condition. Avoid vigorous rubbing, strong fragrances, powders that can irritate skin, or applying medication without advice. Ensure sheets are dry and reasonably smooth, while avoiding tight tucking that creates pressure.

When helping with a transfer, use the method taught by the care team. Friction and shear can occur when skin sticks to bedding while deeper tissue moves. Do not pull on an arm or lift by the armpits. If the patient is heavy, weak, or attached to medical equipment, get help and use approved transfer aids rather than risking a fall or skin injury.

Ask about nutrition, hydration, and support surfaces

Food and fluid needs vary, especially for people with kidney, heart, swallowing, or other medical conditions. Discuss appetite, weight change, swallowing difficulty, and supplements with the treating doctor or dietitian; do not make a high-protein or fluid plan from a general article. Ask whether the mattress and cushions are appropriate for the person's risk and body position. A pressure-relieving surface can support a care plan, but it does not replace regular assessment, repositioning, or skin care.

If equipment is being considered, confirm dimensions, safe setup, cleaning instructions, power needs, and who to contact if it stops working. A nurse or therapist can help the family understand whether a specific surface or cushion fits the patient's needs. Know when to contact a clinician

Contact the care team promptly about a new persistent skin change, blister, open area, increasing pain, warmth, swelling, drainage, or unpleasant smell. Seek urgent medical help for rapidly worsening symptoms, fever, confusion, severe pain, or a person who appears acutely unwell. Do not apply home remedies, antiseptics, or dressings unless the clinician has advised them. Early clinical review can help determine what the skin change means and what care is appropriate.

Build a simple handover routine

Families often share care across relatives, attendants, and nurses. Keep the current position plan, contact numbers, medicines list, and skin concerns in one agreed place. At each handover, mention any missed position change, new discomfort, reduced intake, or change in ability to move. Ask the nurse to demonstrate tasks, then show them back so misunderstandings can be corrected. If you feel unsure, say so; it is safer to pause and ask than to improvise with a wound or transfer.

Prevention works best when the patient is included in decisions and comfort is respected. Agree on a routine that the care team believes is safe, review it after changes in health, and record concerns early. A family member should not be expected to diagnose a wound or provide care beyond their training. Professional review is appropriate whenever the skin is worsening or the care plan is unclear.
